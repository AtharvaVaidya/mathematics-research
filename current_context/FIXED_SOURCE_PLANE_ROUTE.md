# The fixed-source-plane construction route

Date: 24 July 2026

Fix \(x=1\) in the verified three-dimensional Keller map and put
\(t=y+1\).  In the normalization coordinates \((t,c)\), the restricted
target functions become

\[
a=t+t^2-ct^3,\qquad
b=2+4t-3ct^2,\qquad c=c.
\tag{1}
\]

The ring

\[
R=\mathbf Q[a,b,c]\subset\mathbf Q[t,c]
\]

is noninjective.  Indeed, if
\(\theta^2-3\theta-6=0\), the two distinct source points recorded in
`search_fixed_source_plane.py` have \(t\)-coordinates \(1\) and
\(1+\theta\), the same \(c\)-coordinate, and the same values of \(a,b,c\).
Consequently, any \(U,V\in R\) satisfying
\(\operatorname{Jac}_{t,c}(U,V)\in\mathbf Q^\times\) would immediately give
a two-dimensional Keller counterexample.

## 1. Image hypersurface and conductor

Writing the target coordinates as \(A,B,C\), the image is the hypersurface

\[
\Phi=
27A^2C^2-18ABC+16A+B^3C-B^2-3BC-2C+4=0.
\tag{2}
\]

Its normalization is (1).  Define

\[
D=9c^2t^2-12ct-9c+4.
\tag{3}
\]

This is itself a target-ring element:

\[
D=4-3bc-3c.
\tag{4}
\]

The three normalization Jacobians are

\[
\begin{aligned}
m_{ab}&=\{a,b\}=t^2(3ct^2-2t-3),\\
m_{ac}&=\{a,c\}=-3ct^2+2t+1,\\
m_{bc}&=\{b,c\}=-2(3ct-2).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]

They satisfy

\[
\Phi_A=Dm_{bc},\qquad
\Phi_B=-Dm_{ac},\qquad
\Phi_C=Dm_{ab}
\tag{6}
\]

after pullback to the normalization.  Thus \(D=0\) is the conductor/double
curve lying over the singular curve of (2).

There is also a surprisingly small Bézout identity:

\[
\boxed{\quad
1=\frac c2m_{ab}+\frac b2m_{ac}-a m_{bc}.
\quad}
\tag{7}
\]

Therefore the canonical area coefficient already lies in the \(R\)-module
generated by the basic minors.  The unresolved condition is integrability:
the three coefficients in (7) do not themselves arise as the Plücker
coefficients of one exact decomposable form \(dU\wedge dV\).

Equivalently, a Darboux pair must solve the exact Nambu equation

\[
\det(\nabla U,\nabla V,\nabla\Phi)=\pm D
\pmod{\Phi}.
\tag{8}
\]

This is a much smaller formulation of the construction search.

### 1.1 Localized pinch normal form

The conductor admits a useful two-sheeted normal form.  On \(c\ne0\), put
\[
v=3ct-2,\qquad x=v^2,\qquad y=v(x-9c).
\]
Then
\[
b=\frac{4+6c-x}{3c},\qquad
a=\frac{18c-3x+4-y}{27c^2},
\]
and hence
\[
R[c^{-1}]
=\mathbf Q[c^{\pm1},x,y]\big/\left(y^2-x(x-9c)^2\right).
\tag{8a}
\]
The conductor is \(x=9c,\ y=0\), and its two normalization branches are
exchanged by \(v\mapsto-v\).  This involution fixes \(b,c\) and satisfies
\[
a(-v)-a(v)=\frac{2v(x-9c)}{27c^2}.
\]
Thus (8a) is a localized pinch presentation, not merely a parametrization.

It also converts the full Darboux equation into a pair of boundary
identities.  Write arbitrary elements as
\[
U=F(c,x)+yG(c,x),\qquad
V=P(c,x)+yQ(c,x).
\]
Because
\(\operatorname{Jac}_{t,c}=3c\,\operatorname{Jac}_{v,c}\), a bracket
normalized to \(1\) has
\(\operatorname{Jac}_{v,c}(U,V)=1/(3c)\).  Taking the odd and even parts
on \(x=9c\) gives
\[
F_xP_c-F_cP_x=0,
\tag{8b}
\]
\[
G(P_c+9P_x)-Q(F_c+9F_x)=\frac1{54c^2}.
\tag{8c}
\]
Equation (8b) says that the even-part Jacobian is divisible by the
conductor; (8c) is a one-variable Bézout identity for the odd normal jets.
These two equations are a structural boundary problem for arbitrary
\(U,V\in R\), and are a more promising route than increasing target-degree
bounds.

There is a compatible exact symplectic boundary condition.  Since
\(dU\wedge dV=dt\wedge dc\), the closed polynomial form
\(U\,dV-t\,dc\) equals \(dS\) for some \(S\in\mathbf Q[t,c]\).  On the
conductor, parametrized by
\[
c=v^2/9,\qquad t=3(v+2)/v^2,
\]
the two branches have the same \(U,V\), and consequently
\[
S(v)-S(-v)=-\frac43v.
\tag{8d}
\]
The presentation and all three boundary identities are checked in
`verify_fixed_plane_conductor_normal_form.py`.

### 1.2 A target-ring Liouville form

The normalized area form has no obstruction at the level of ordinary
algebraic de Rham exactness.  In target coordinates put
\[
\begin{aligned}
p&=\frac{27AC^2-15BC+16}{6},\\
q&=\frac{3B^2C-4B-3C}{12},\\
r&=\frac{12AB-B^3+3B+2}{12}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Then the target-ring one-form
\[
\alpha=p\,dA+q\,dB+r\,dC
\]
pulls back to the particularly simple form
\[
\alpha=-2c\,dt-t\,dc,\qquad d\alpha=dt\wedge dc. \tag{8e}
\]
Thus the remaining obstruction is strictly stronger than a de Rham
class: one must decide whether a primitive of (8e), after adding an exact
form, can factor as \(U\,dV\) with \(U,V\in R\).

The same calculation isolates the normalization denominator:
\[
D=4-3bc-3c,\qquad N=b-2-9ca=tD,
\qquad t=\frac ND. \tag{8f}
\]
The obvious canonical primitive \(t\,dc\) therefore fails to descend
precisely at the conductor.  Equations (8e)--(8f) turn the fixed-plane
problem into a polynomial Liouville-factorization problem rather than a
search for an arbitrary closed form.  They are checked in
`verify_fixed_plane_liouville_form.py`.

### 1.3 Exact residue condition and a formal conductor solution

The primitive parity condition has a sharper one-variable form.  Put
\[
u(c)=U|_{x=9c,y=0},\qquad
w(c)=V|_{x=9c,y=0}.
\]
These are Laurent polynomials in \(c\).  The average of the two branch
restrictions of \(U\,dV=dS+t\,dc\), using
\[
\frac{t(v)+t(-v)}2=\frac2{3c},
\]
gives
\[
u(c)w'(c)=s_0'(c)+\frac2{3c},
\tag{8g}
\]
where \(s_0\in\mathbf Q[c^{\pm1}]\) is the even part of the conductor
restriction of \(S\).  Consequently every global Darboux pair must obey
\[
\boxed{\operatorname {Res}_{c=0}\bigl(u\,dw\bigr)=\frac23.}
\tag{8h}
\]
This is the cyclic/de Rham content of (8d), not an ordinary area-form
obstruction.

The first-jet equations also simplify.  Write \(s=x-9c\) and set, on
\(s=0\),
\[
f=F_x,\quad p=P_x,\quad g=G,\quad q=Q.
\]
Since \(u'=F_c+9F_x\) and \(w'=P_c+9P_x\), (8b)--(8c) become
\[
fw'-pu'=0,\qquad gw'-qu'=\frac1{54c^2}.
\tag{8i}
\]
In particular \(u'\) and \(w'\) generate the unit ideal in
\(\mathbf Q[c^{\pm1}]\), and the first equation then forces
\((f,p)=h(u',w')\) for some Laurent polynomial \(h\).

These necessary conditions are consistent in the strongest possible
formal sense.  In fact, they extend to an exact Darboux pair in the
completed pinch ring.  In the normalization put
\[
s=v^2-9c,\qquad x=v^2,\qquad
p_0=\frac13\log\left(1-\frac{s}{v^2}\right).
\]
The logarithm is its \(s\)-adic power series with zero constant term.  The
completion of the target ring along its conductor is
\[
\widehat R
=\mathbf Q[v^{\pm2}]
 s\,\mathbf Q[v^{\pm1}][[s]],
\]
and the two series
\[
\boxed{
U=x,\qquad
V=\frac{p_0}{2v}-\frac2{3x}
=-\frac2{3x}
-\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{y\,s^{n-1}}{6n\,x^{n+1}}
}
\tag{8j}
\]
belong to \(\widehat R\).  Since
\[
c=\frac{v^2-s}{9},\qquad
t=\frac{3(v+2)}{v^2-s},
\]
direct differentiation gives
\[
dU\wedge dV
=-\frac{dv\wedge ds}{3(v^2-s)}
=dt\wedge dc.
\tag{8k}
\]
Moreover this solution satisfies the exact, rather than merely closed,
Liouville equation
\[
U\,dV-t\,dc=dS,\qquad
S=-\frac{v+4}{2}p_0-\frac23v.
\tag{8l}
\]
At \(s=0\), \(S=-2v/3\), so (8d) holds exactly.  Its boundary values are
\[
u=9c,\qquad w=-\frac2{27c},
\]
and hence \(\operatorname {Res}(u\,dw)=2/3\).

There is even an explicit formal deck involution:
\[
\iota(v,s)=
\left(-v,-\frac{s v^2}{v^2-s}\right).
\tag{8m}
\]
It is an involution, extends \(v\mapsto-v\) on the conductor, reverses the
normal coordinate to first order, sends \(p_0\mapsto-p_0\), and fixes both
series in (8j).

Thus the conductor exact sequence, formal deck symmetry, parity, and
residue conditions cannot by themselves yield an all-degree
contradiction.  The obstruction, if this route is impossible globally,
must be an **algebraization/finite-support obstruction**: the logarithmic
tail in (8j) has infinitely many nonzero terms.  This exact formal solution
and all displayed identities are checked in
`verify_fixed_plane_formal_conductor_solution.py`.

### 1.4 What algebraization must obstruct

The logarithm in (8j) is forced if one fixes \(U=x\), and more generally
if \(U=h(x)\).  Indeed, regarding \(v,s\) as independent coordinates,
\[
\operatorname {Jac}_{v,s}(U,V)
=2v h'(v^2)V_s
=-\frac1{3(v^2-s)}.
\]
Hence
\[
V_s=-\frac1{6v h'(v^2)(v^2-s)}.
\tag{8n}
\]
As a rational function of \(s\), the right side has a nonzero simple-pole
residue at \(s=v^2\).  A derivative of a rational function has zero
residue at every pole.  Thus \(h(x)\) has no rational Darboux mate, and
formal integration of (8n) necessarily introduces
\[
\frac1{6v h'(v^2)}
\log\left(1-\frac{s}{v^2}\right).
\]

This logarithm is **not** forced for an arbitrary Hamiltonian.  There is
an explicit rational pair in the target fraction field that descends
across the conductor and passes (8h).  Start from the rational canonical
pair
\[
q=cv,\qquad p=-\frac1{3c},
\qquad dq\wedge dp=dt\wedge dc,
\]
and make the monomial canonical change
\[
Q=q^3p^4=\frac{v^3}{81c},
\qquad
P=-q^{-2}p^{-3}=\frac{27c}{x}.
\]
On the conductor \(P=3\) and \(Q\) is odd.  Therefore
\[
A=Q^2=\frac{x^3}{6561c^2},\qquad
B_0=\frac{P-3}{2Q}
=-\frac{243cy}{2x^3}
\]
both descend, and \(dA\wedge dB_0=dt\wedge dc\).  The canonical shear
\[
B=B_0-\frac2{3A}
\tag{8o}
\]
preserves the area equation.  Its boundary values are
\[
A|_C=\frac c9,\qquad B|_C=-\frac6c,
\]
so \(\operatorname {Res}(A\,dB)=2/3\), exactly as required.

The failure is now global and sharply visible:
\[
A\,dB-t\,dc
=d\left(-\frac56v+\frac{v^3}{54c}\right)
+2\,d\log\left(\frac xc\right).
\tag{8p}
\]
The logarithmic class is nonzero rationally; for example its coefficient
has residue \(4\) along \(v=0\).  Yet \(x/c=9\) on the conductor, so this
same logarithm has a perfectly valid power-series primitive in the
conductor completion.  Thus neither formal exactness nor rational
algebraicity of \(U,V\) is the missing condition.

The remaining candidate obstruction is a global residue theorem:

\[
\boxed{\text{show that every descended rational Darboux pair satisfying
(8h) retains a nonzero logarithmic residue away from the conductor.}}
\tag{8q}
\]

A polynomial pair would make \(U\,dV-t\,dc\) a polynomial exact form, so
all such residues would have to vanish.  The special rational model and
its class (8p) are checked in
`verify_fixed_plane_rational_conductor_model.py`.

### 1.5 Residue exact sequence and the odd unit lattice

The rational model identifies exactly what the conductor completion
forgets.  On
\[
X^\circ=\operatorname {Spec}\mathbf Q[v^{\pm1},c^{\pm1}]
\]
one has
\[
H^1_{\mathrm {dR}}(X^\circ)
=\mathbf Q\,d\log v\oplus\mathbf Q\,d\log c.
\]
Restriction to the punctured conductor \(c=v^2/9\) is
\[
(r_v,r_c)\longmapsto r_v+2r_c.
\tag{8r}
\]
Consequently
\[
\ker\left(H^1_{\mathrm {dR}}(X^\circ)
\longrightarrow H^1_{\mathrm {dR}}(C^\circ)\right)
=\mathbf Q\,d\log\left(\frac{x}{c}\right).
\tag{8s}
\]
Formal conductor exactness detects only the quotient by this kernel.  The
class in (8p) is \(2\,d\log(x/c)\), so it is invisible after restriction.
In residue language it has
\[
\operatorname {Res}_{v=0}=4,\qquad
\operatorname {Res}_{c=0}=-2,
\]
and residue \(-2\) on the line at infinity in the projective
compactification.  The first two contributions cancel under (8r).

There is a complete parity calculation for the natural monomial family
of rational canonical charts.  Continue to write
\[
q=cv,\qquad p=-\frac1{3c}.
\]
For any integer \(k\ne0\), put
\[
Q_k=q^{1+2k}p^{1+3k},\qquad
P_k=-\frac1kq^{-2k}p^{-3k}.
\tag{8t}
\]
The exponent determinant is \(-k\), so
\(dQ_k\wedge dP_k=dq\wedge dp\).  On the conductor,
\[
Q_k=\text{constant}\cdot v,\qquad
P_k=\text{constant}.
\]
Thus the same quadratic quotient
\[
A_k=Q_k^2,\qquad
B_k=\frac{P_k-P_k|_C}{2Q_k}-\frac2{3A_k}
\tag{8u}
\]
is rational, descends, has the normalized area form, and satisfies the
boundary residue (8h).

The Liouville class can be computed without expansion.  For monomial
canonical coordinates,
\[
[Q_k\,dP_k]=[q\,dp],
\]
while the quadratic quotient and final shear give
\[
[A_k\,dB_k]=[Q_k\,dP_k]+\frac43[d\log Q_k].
\]
Since
\[
[q\,dp-t\,dc]=-\frac23[d\log c],
\qquad
Q_k=\text{constant}\cdot v^{1+2k}c^{-k},
\]
one obtains
\[
\boxed{
[A_k\,dB_k-t\,dc]
=\frac{2(2k+1)}3
\left[d\log\left(\frac{x}{c}\right)\right].
}
\tag{8v}
\]
This class cannot vanish for an integer \(k\); cancellation would require
\(k=-1/2\), precisely the logarithmic resonant chart \(Q\sim v\).
The opposite monomial orientation \(Q|_C\sim v^{-1}\) gives the negative
class
\[
-\frac{2(2k+1)}3[d\log(x/c)]
\]
and has the same forbidden half-integral zero.  Thus allowing either
conductor-adapted monomial orientation does not produce cancellation inside
one rational chart.  This full two-orientation monomial family has a
nonzero global residue, even though every member passes all conductor
tests.

For a general rational pair with pole divisor \(E=\bigcup E_i\), the
closed Liouville form has a residue vector
\[
\left(\rho_{E_i}\right)\in\bigoplus_i\mathbf Q[E_i].
\]
Rational exactness forces every \(\rho_{E_i}\) to vanish, while conductor
exactness imposes only the pushforward/restriction relations
\[
\sum_i\operatorname {ord}_p(E_i|_C)\rho_{E_i}=0
\quad\text{at every }p\in\overline C.
\tag{8w}
\]
Equation (8v) proves the desired nonvanishing on the full monomial
subgroup.  The remaining all-degree gap is now a precise unit-lattice
lemma: prove that an arbitrary descended rational Darboux chart satisfying
(8h) belongs to the same odd residue coset, or exhibit a nonmonomial chart
that cancels it.  The restriction map and (8v) are checked symbolically in
`verify_fixed_plane_residue_lattice.py`.  The conjectural mod-two
square-class formulation, its invariance under elementary symplectic
postcompositions, and the precise nonmonomial gap are recorded in
`FIXED_PLANE_RESIDUE_PARITY.md`.

### 1.6 The cuspidal endpoint after compactification

The global residue gap can be seen geometrically.  Compactify the source
in \(\mathbf P^1_t\times\mathbf P^1_c\).  The conductor has bidegree
\((2,2)\) and two endpoints:
\[
(t,c)=(\infty,0),\qquad (0,\infty).
\]
The first is a cusp.  Blow it up using
\[
\tau=t^{-1},\qquad c=r\tau.
\]
Then
\[
a=\frac{1-r}{\tau^2}+\frac1\tau,\qquad
b=\frac{4-3r}{\tau}+2,\qquad c=r\tau,
\tag{8x}
\]
and the strict conductor is
\[
(3r-2)^2-9r\tau=0.
\tag{8y}
\]
Its normalization is
\[
r=\frac{v+2}{3},\qquad
\tau=\frac{v^2}{3(v+2)}.
\]
Thus it is smooth after the first blowup but tangent with multiplicity two
to the exceptional divisor \(\tau=0\) at \(r=2/3\).  Two further point
blowups separate the conductor and the boundary divisors.

The source forms become especially transparent:
\[
dt\wedge dc=-\frac{d\tau}{\tau}\wedge dr,\qquad
t\,dc=r\frac{d\tau}{\tau}+dr.
\tag{8z}
\]
The residue \(r=2/3\) on the first exceptional divisor pulls back with
tangency two to the conductor residue \(4/3\) at \(v=0\).  This is the
compactified origin of (8h).

There is no obstruction from these divisors alone.  For any nonzero
integer \(m\),
\[
U=\tau^m,\qquad
V=-\frac{r}{m\tau^m}
\]
satisfy
\[
dU\wedge dV=dt\wedge dc,
\]
and
\[
U\,dV-t\,dc
=d\left(-\left(1+\frac1m\right)r\right).
\tag{8aa}
\]
This rational exact chart has precisely the required log-symplectic pole
divisor.  It fails the fixed-plane problem because its restrictions do
not satisfy the branch descent \(v\mapsto-v\).  Consequently canonical
divisors, intersection negativity, and endpoint residues by themselves
cannot prove impossibility; the all-degree theorem must combine the
endpoint valuation with the target-ring parity in (8x).  The blowups and
the counterpattern (8aa) are checked in
`verify_fixed_plane_endpoint_blowup.py`.

### 1.7 A balanced endpoint obstruction in all degrees

The first endpoint filtration does use both global target-ring regularity
and conductor descent.  Grade by pole order along \(\tau=0\).  From (8x)
the three target initial forms are
\[
A_0=(1-r)\tau^{-2},\qquad
B_0=(4-3r)\tau^{-1},\qquad
C_0=r\tau,
\]
of degrees \(2,1,-1\).  Every degree-zero monomial satisfies
\(\gamma=2\alpha+\beta\), so
\[
(\operatorname {gr}R)_0
=\mathbf Q[f,g],\qquad
f=A_0C_0^2=r^2(1-r),\qquad
g=B_0C_0=r(4-3r).
\tag{8ab}
\]
At the conductor tangency \(r_0=2/3\),
\[
f'(r_0)=g'(r_0)=0.
\tag{8ac}
\]
Thus every degree-zero initial form has derivative zero at \(r_0\).

Suppose a Darboux pair has opposite nonzero endpoint degrees \(p,-p\).
Write its leading forms as
\[
U_0=\tau^{-p}u_0(r),\qquad
V_0=\tau^p v_0(r).
\]
The coefficient of \(\tau^{-1}\) in their Jacobian is
\[
-p\,(u_0v_0)'.
\]
Since \(dU\wedge dV=-\tau^{-1}d\tau\wedge dr\), it would have to be the
nonzero constant \(-1\).  But \(u_0v_0\in(\operatorname {gr}R)_0\), so
(8ac) makes this coefficient vanish at \(r_0\).  Contradiction.
Therefore
\[
\boxed{\nu_E(U)+\nu_E(V)=0,\quad \nu_E(U)\ne0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{no Darboux pair}.}
\tag{8ad}
\]
This is an all-degree endpoint theorem, not a bounded search.  The graded
ring and Jacobian coefficient are checked in
`verify_fixed_plane_endpoint_graded.py`.

The same endpoint calculation excludes every valuation regime except one.
If \(H=\tau^{-d}h(r)\) is any homogeneous element of
\(\operatorname {gr}R\), then
\[
2h'(2/3)+3d\,h(2/3)=0.
\tag{8ae}
\]
This holds on \(A_0,B_0,C_0\) and is preserved by products and sums of
fixed degree.

Let \(p,q\) be the largest endpoint degrees of \(U,V\).

- If \(p+q<0\), no pair of terms can produce the required degree-zero
  Jacobian coefficient.
- If \(p+q=0\), (8ad) handles the nonzero case.  For \(p=q=0\), both
  leading \(\tau\)-derivatives vanish, so the degree-zero coefficient is
  again zero.
- If \(p>0>q=-s\), top cancellation gives
  \[
  p\,u_0v_0'+s\,u_0'v_0=0,
  \]
  so \((u_0^s v_0^p)'=0\).  Polynomiality makes both \(u_0,v_0\)
  constants, contradicting (8ae) in their nonzero degrees.
- If \(p>0,q=0\), top cancellation makes the degree-zero leading
  coefficient of \(V\) constant.  Subtracting that constant lowers \(q\)
  to the already-excluded mixed-sign or nonpositive cases.

Consequently every surviving Darboux pair must have
\[
\boxed{p>0,\qquad q>0.}
\tag{8af}
\]
Its top coefficient equation is
\[
p\,u_0v_0'-q\,u_0'v_0=0.
\]
Writing \(d=\gcd(p,q)\), unique factorization in \(\mathbf Q[r]\) gives
\[
u_0=\alpha h^{p/d},\qquad
v_0=\beta h^{q/d}
\tag{8ag}
\]
for a common approximate root \(h\in\mathbf Q[r]\).

Centering \(w=r-2/3\) makes the nonnormality exact:
\[
g-\frac43=-3w^2,\qquad
f-\frac4{27}=-w^2-w^3,
\]
so
\[
(\operatorname {gr}R)_0=\mathbf Q[w^2,w^3].
\tag{8ah}
\]
For every positive degree \(e\), the coefficient module is in fact
\[
(\operatorname {gr}R)_e
=\tau^{-e}
\left\{h(w)\in\mathbf Q[w]:
2h'(0)+3e\,h(0)=0\right\}.
\tag{8ai}
\]
The inclusion from left to right is (8ae).  For the converse, the degree
one identities
\[
A_0C_0-\frac19B_0=-w^2\tau^{-1}
\]
and the degree two identity
\[
B_0^2-12A_0=9w^2\tau^{-2}
\]
start the conductor ideal \(w^2\mathbf Q[w]\) in degrees one and two.
Multiplication by the coprime leading coefficients of \(A_0\) and \(B_0\)
propagates it to every positive degree.  A power of \(B_0\) supplies the
unique allowed constant/linear jet.

Consequently the homogeneous approximate root
\(\tau^{-d}h(w)\) in (8ag) fails to lie in
\(\operatorname {gr}R\) precisely when \(h\) has a simple zero at \(w=0\).
Indeed, a nonzero constant term makes the Robin condition descend from
any power to \(h\), while a zero of order at least two lies in the
conductor ideal in (8ai).  The missing simple root is the cusp defect
\(w\notin\mathbf Q[w^2,w^3]\).

There is a second, independent issue.  If one of the coprime exponents
\(p/d,q/d\) equals one, an area-preserving shear
\(V\mapsto V-\lambda U^k\), or its symmetric version, cancels the top
term and lowers one endpoint degree.  Iterating either reaches an
excluded sign regime or a reduced pair.  A reduced survivor therefore
has
\[
\boxed{
p=da,\quad q=db,\quad
\gcd(a,b)=1,\quad a,b\ge2,
}
\tag{8aj}
\]
with leading coefficients \(\alpha h^a,\beta h^b\).  The root \(h\) may
be a genuine element of the positive graded piece or the simple
nonnormal root above.  Thus the exact remaining endpoint gap is the
**positive-positive nondivisible approximate-root case**, with an
additional cusp-root subcase.

### 1.8 The second endpoint and the linked cusp roots

The other compactification endpoint gives an independent filtration.  Put
\[
z=c^{-1}
\]
near \((t,c)=(0,\infty)\).  Then
\[
a=t+t^2-\frac{t^3}{z},\qquad
b=2+4t-\frac{3t^2}{z},\qquad
c=\frac1z,
\]
and
\[
dt\wedge dc=-z^{-2}dt\wedge dz.
\tag{8ak}
\]
All three target initial forms have \(z\)-pole degree one:
\[
-t^3z^{-1},\qquad -3t^2z^{-1},\qquad z^{-1}.
\]
The coefficient of a degree-\(n\) target monomial is
\[
t^{3\alpha+2\beta},\qquad
\alpha+\beta\le n.
\tag{8al}
\]
Thus every positive-degree leading coefficient lies in the cusp ring
\(\mathbf Q[t^2,t^3]\) and has derivative zero at \(t=0\).

If the two second-endpoint pole degrees sum to one, the only nonnegative
possibilities are \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\).  The degree-zero leading
coefficient is constant, so the \(z^{-2}\) Jacobian coefficient vanishes.
If the sum is greater than one, top cancellation again forces common
approximate-root powers.  Hence this endpoint has the same missing-linear-
parameter phenomenon as (8ah), now with hidden root \(t\).

The two cusp defects are not independent.  In the normalization define
\[
H=3ct^2-2t-3.
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{
H^2=(b+1)^2-12a,\qquad H+b+1=2t.
}
\tag{8am}
\]
At the first endpoint,
\[
H=3\frac{w-\tau}{\tau},
\]
so its initial form is the missing root \(w\tau^{-1}\).  At the second
endpoint, \(b+1\) has quadratic conductor order and (8am) identifies the
missing root \(t\) with \(H/2\) to first order.  Any surviving
nondivisible approximate-root pattern must therefore be compatible with
the same global square root \(H\) at both endpoints.

For a target monomial \(a^\alpha b^\beta c^\gamma\), its two endpoint
degrees lie in the cone generated by
\[
(2,1),\qquad(1,1),\qquad(-1,1),
\tag{8an}
\]
where the first coordinate is the cusp pole degree and the second is the
degree at \(c=\infty\).  In particular
\[
-n\le p\le2n.
\]
The remaining two-valuation problem is to show that the reduced coprime
exponents in (8aj) cannot be realized compatibly at both linked cusps.
Writing
\[
U=z^{-p}F(t,z),\qquad V=z^{-q}G(t,z)
\]
turns this into the exact deformation equation
\[
\begin{aligned}
pFG_t-qF_tG
+z(F_tG_z-F_zG_t)
=-z^{p+q-1}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{8ao}
\]
Here \(F,G\) lie in the cusp deformation generated by
\[
za=-t^3+z(t+t^2),\qquad
z(b-2)=-3t^2+4zt,\qquad z.
\]
Modulo \(z\), (8ao) is precisely the common-root equation in
\(\mathbf Q[t^2,t^3]\).  For reduced data
\[
F=t^a+\cdots,\qquad G=t^b+\cdots,\qquad
p=da,\quad q=db,
\]
a correction appearing only at the final right-hand-side order would
have to solve
\[
t^{a-1}\bigl(ptg'+a(p-1)g\bigr)=-1,
\]
which is polynomially impossible for \(a\ge2\).  The exact surviving
question is whether the forced earlier \(z\)-corrections can lower the
\(t\)-order enough before order \(p+q-1\).  Equation (8ao), rather than a
bounded coefficient ansatz, is the next all-degree recurrence.
The second endpoint and identities (8am) are checked in
`verify_fixed_plane_second_endpoint.py`.

### 1.9 Exact linked-cusp Rees algebra

The deformation in (8ao) has a small exact presentation.  Put
\[
X=za,\qquad Y=z(b-2).
\]
Then its image is
\[
\begin{aligned}
0=R={}&Y^3+27X^2+z(-18XY+6Y^2)\\
&+z^2(-36X-Y^2+9Y)+z^3(16X-4Y).
\end{aligned}
\tag{8ap}
\]
The normalization is \(\mathbf Q[t,z]\), with
\[
X=-t^3+z(t+t^2),\qquad Y=-3t^2+4zt.
\]
Define
\[
\delta=4z^2-3Y-9z,\qquad n=Yz-9X.
\]
Their normalization pullbacks obey
\[
\delta=9t^2-12tz-9z+4z^2,\qquad n=t\delta.
\tag{8aq}
\]
Moreover the conductor is exactly \((\delta,n)\).  Indeed,
\(\mathbf Q[t,z]\) is generated as a module by \(1,t\), the quotient by
\((\delta,n)\) is \(\mathbf Q[z]\), and on the normalized conductor the
two roots are exchanged by
\[
t\longmapsto \frac{4z}{3}-t.
\tag{8ar}
\]
Thus descent at the second cusp is an exact parity condition modulo
\(\delta\), rather than only the initial cusp equation modulo \(z\).

There is also an exact corner map between the two endpoint filtrations.
For a target monomial \(a^\alpha b^\beta c^\gamma\), put
\[
e=2\alpha+\beta-\gamma,\qquad
m=\alpha+\beta+\gamma.
\]
Its second-endpoint coefficient is a scalar times
\[
t^{3\alpha+2\beta}=t^{e+m}.
\tag{8as}
\]
That scalar is also the coefficient of \(w^m\) in its first-endpoint
coefficient
\[
\left(\frac13-w\right)^\alpha
(2-3w)^\beta
\left(\frac23+w\right)^\gamma,
\]
so a nonzero corner of bidegree \((e,m)\) is simultaneously a monomial
in the hidden root at both cusps.

Crucially, this linkage does **not** supply the desired contradiction.
There are explicit target-ring elements
\[
\begin{aligned}
P_2&=\frac{(b+1)^2-12a}{9}=\frac{H^2}{9},\\
P_3&=-\frac23ab+\frac1{27}b^3+2a^2c,
\end{aligned}
\tag{8at}
\]
whose simultaneous endpoint initials are
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
&(\infty,0)&(0,\infty)\\ \hline
P_2&w^2\tau^{-2}&t^4z^{-2}\\
P_3&w^3\tau^{-3}&t^6z^{-3}.
\end{array}
\tag{8au}
\]
Products \(P_2^\alpha P_3^\beta\) therefore realize every linked hidden
power \(H^n\) in the double associated graded ring for
\(n=2\alpha+3\beta\), hence every \(n\ge2\).  Even the fact that one of
the coprime exponents in (8aj) is odd is therefore compatible with exact
global target-ring descent: lower filtration terms repair the sign
defect.

The pair \(P_2,P_3\) is not itself Darboux:
\[
\{P_2,P_3\}
=\frac4{27}t^2(3ct^2-2t-4)H.
\tag{8av}
\]
Thus (8at)--(8av) construct a genuine all-degree **leading survivor**,
not a Keller pair.  They rule out a closure based only on the two cusp
semigroups or the global sign line.  A successful endpoint proof must use
the lower-order symplectic recurrence (8ao), equivalently the exact
normal jet transverse to the conductor.

Equations (8ap)--(8av) are checked in
`verify_fixed_plane_linked_cusp_semigroup.py`.

### 1.10 Conductor cokernel and its second normal layer

The exact conductor presentation makes (8ao) into a finite cokernel
problem.  Every descended numerator has a unique form modulo the
conductor
\[
F=f(z)+\delta A(t,z),\qquad
G=g(z)+\delta B(t,z),
\tag{8aw}
\]
with \(f,g\in\mathbf Q[z]\).  Put
\[
\eta=\delta_t=18t-12z,\qquad
c_A=zg'-qg,\qquad c_B=pf-zf'.
\]
Since
\[
\eta^2\equiv324z\pmod\delta,
\tag{8ax}
\]
reduction of (8ao) modulo \(\delta\) is exactly
\[
\eta(c_AA+c_BB)=-z^{p+q-1}.
\tag{8ay}
\]
Writing
\[
A=A_0+A_1\eta,\qquad B=B_0+B_1\eta
\quad\pmod\delta
\]
splits (8ay) into
\[
\begin{aligned}
c_AA_0+c_BB_0&=0,\\
c_AA_1+c_BB_1&=-\frac1{324}z^{p+q-2}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{8az}
\]
Consequently the first exact obstruction is the class
\[
\left[z^{p+q-2}\right]\in
\mathbf Q[z]/(zg'-qg,\;pf-zf').
\tag{8ba}
\]
In particular the two scaled conductor derivatives can have no common
zero away from \(z=0\), and their common \(z\)-order is at most
\(p+q-2\).  This is the endpoint refinement of the unit-ideal condition
in (8i).

If
\[
f=z^pf_0,\qquad g=z^qg_0,
\]
then the two generators simplify to
\[
c_A=z^{q+1}g_0',\qquad c_B=-z^{p+1}f_0'.
\tag{8bb}
\]
For the linked square/cube leading survivor (8at), \(p=2,q=3\) and
\[
f=\frac49z^3,\qquad
g=\frac1{27}z^3(4z-1).
\]
Thus
\[
c_A=\frac4{27}z^4,\qquad
c_B=-\frac49z^3.
\tag{8bc}
\]
The ideal in (8ba) is exactly \((z^3)\), while the required right hand
side also has order \(p+q-2=3\).  The survivor therefore saturates,
rather than violates, the first conductor cokernel.  At the saturated
scalar, (8az) requires
\[
B_1(0)=\frac1{144}
\tag{8bd}
\]
when \(A_1\) is regular.  The particular \(P_3\) in (8at) has
\(B_1(0)=0\), so that explicit pair does not pass the scalar equation.
This scalar is linked back to the cusp initial by
\[
F(t,0)=f(0)+9A_0(0)t^2+162A_1(0)t^3+O(t^4).
\tag{8be}
\]
Hence changing the saturated scalar changes a low cusp coefficient; it
cannot be regarded as a free conductor correction once the complete
second-endpoint numerator and the conductor boundary data are fixed.  In
particular (8bd) forces a nonzero \(t^3\) term in the cube numerator,
whereas the particular \(P_3\) in (8au) has initial \(t^6\).  Terms
starting with \(\delta^2\) have \(t\)-order at least four at \(z=0\), so
they cannot repair this mismatch while \(f,g\) remain fixed.

This does not exclude every pair with the same two leading corners.
For example, adding
\[
\lambda c^{p-1}
\]
to a component of second pole degree \(p>1\) preserves both endpoint
leading forms but changes its scaled conductor restriction by
\(\lambda z\).  The corresponding generator \(pf-zf'\) then has order
one, so the cokernel is no longer saturated and no constant odd jet is
forced.  Thus (8bd) excludes the rigid \(P_2,P_3\) boundary lift, not its
associated-graded corner.

For completeness, the forced-low-jet argument has a sharp range on the
unperturbed canonical linked semigroup.  On the conductor,
\[
P_2=\frac49z,\qquad P_3=\frac{4z-1}{27}.
\]
For
\[
P_n=P_2^\alpha P_3^\beta,\qquad n=2\alpha+3\beta,
\]
put \(f_n=z^nP_n\).  Direct differentiation gives
\[
\operatorname {ord}_z(nf_n-zf_n')=
\begin{cases}
n+\alpha,&\alpha>0,\\
n+1,&\alpha=0.
\end{cases}
\tag{8bea}
\]
Using the least \(\alpha\in\{0,1,2\}\) with
\(2\alpha\equiv n\pmod3\), this order is \(n+1\), except for
\(n\equiv1\pmod3\), when it is \(n+2\).  For coprime
\(2\le a<b\), equality with the required order \(a+b-2\) in (8az)
forces \((a,b)=(2,3)\).  Thus the nonzero \(t^3\)-jet contradiction
rejects exactly the minimal square/cube **boundary lift** in this
unperturbed family.  Larger coprime lifts can satisfy the first equation
with an odd jet vanishing to positive \(z\)-order, while lower terms such
as \(c^{p-1}\) also desaturate the minimal case.  Excluding the corner
itself would require a lower bound on boundary derivative ideals that is
stable under all such lower-filtration corrections; no such bound is
currently available.

There is a canonical next obstruction.  Write
\[
F=f+\delta A+\delta^2C,\qquad
G=g+\delta B+\delta^2E.
\]
Modulo \(\delta^2\), the recurrence operator has the form
\[
\mathcal L
=\mathcal L_0(A,B)
+\delta\bigl(\mathcal L_1(A,B)+2\mathcal L_0(C,E)\bigr),
\tag{8bf}
\]
where
\[
\mathcal L_0(A,B)=\eta(c_AA+c_BB)
\]
and
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_1(A,B)={}&
p(fB_t+A\eta B)-q(\eta AB+A_tg)\\
&+z\{\eta AB_z+A_t(g'+\delta_zB)
-(f'+\delta_zA)B_t-A_z\eta B\}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{8bg}
\]
After a solution of (8ay) is chosen, divide
\(\mathcal L_0+z^{p+q-1}\) by \(\delta\).  Its sum with
\(\mathcal L_1\) defines a Bockstein class in
\[
\frac{\mathbf Q[z]}{z(c_A,c_B)}
\ \oplus\
\eta\,\frac{\mathbf Q[z]}{(c_A,c_B)}.
\tag{8bh}
\]
Vanishing of this class is necessary and sufficient for a lift through
the second normal layer.  Formula (8bh) is the appropriate finite target
for the next structural calculation; unlike the endpoint semigroup, it
retains the transverse symplectic jet.

For the saturated boundary data (8bc), every first solution can be
written
\[
A=a_0+a_1\eta,\qquad
B=\frac z3A+\frac{\eta}{144}
\quad\pmod\delta.
\]
Its second Bockstein class is represented by
\[
\begin{aligned}
\rho_{\rm even}
&=-\frac98a_0z-\frac94a_0'z^2-\frac16z^3,\\
\rho_{\rm odd}
&=-\frac94z(a_1+za_1').
\end{aligned}
\tag{8bi}
\]
This layer is also not a universal obstruction: choosing
\[
a_0=-\frac4{135}z^2,\qquad a_1=0
\]
makes both representatives vanish identically.  Thus the first scalar
excludes only the rigid square/cube boundary lift, and after relaxing its
normal jet the second normal layer can be lifted.  Any all-degree closure
must track the cusp initial, boundary Laurent data, and conductor
Bockstein simultaneously, not any one filtration separately.

Equations (8aw)--(8bi) are checked in
`verify_fixed_plane_conductor_bockstein.py`.

### 1.11 Exact boundary-filtration image and the local limitation

The lower-term loophole above is universal.  On the conductor,
\[
a=\frac{4-9c}{27c^2},\qquad
b=\frac{4-3c}{3c},\qquad
b+1=\frac4{3c}.
\tag{8bj}
\]
Let \(\mathcal F_pR\) be the second-endpoint pole filtration and define
the scaled restriction
\[
\rho_p(U)=z^pU|_{\rm conductor},\qquad z=c^{-1}.
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{\quad
\rho_p(\mathcal F_pR)=\mathbf Q[z]_{\le2p}.
\quad}
\tag{8bk}
\]
Indeed, \(c^j\), \(0\le j\le p\), supplies
\[
z^p c^j=z^{p-j},
\]
while \((b+1)^k\), \(0\le k\le p\), supplies a nonzero scalar multiple
of
\[
z^{p+k}.
\]
These give every monomial from \(1\) through \(z^{2p}\).  If the cusp
initial forces the constant term to vanish, the image is correspondingly
\(z\mathbf Q[z]_{\le2p-1}\).

Consequently, for every surviving pole degree \(p>1\), adding the lower
term
\[
\lambda c^{p-1}
\tag{8bl}
\]
does not change the pole-\(p\) initial at the second endpoint and has
negative first-endpoint degree, so it also leaves the positive first
corner unchanged.  But it changes the scaled boundary numerator by
\[
\Delta f=\lambda z
\]
and hence changes the cokernel generator by
\[
\Delta(pf-zf')=(p-1)\lambda z.
\tag{8bm}
\]
For generic \(\lambda\), the boundary derivative ideal therefore has
\(z\)-order one.  Since \(p,q\ge2\) in the only surviving regime, the
required class \(z^{p+q-2}\) in (8ba) is automatically divisible by this
adjustable generator.

Thus neither the two endpoint semigroups nor the first conductor
cokernel can be a universal obstruction: lower global target terms
preserve all leading data while changing the boundary normal equation.
The Bockstein tower remains a valid exact bookkeeping device, but an
all-degree proof cannot come from a finite leading-corner/residue class
that ignores these lower terms.  A successful route must impose a
genuinely global nonlinear condition, such as the critical-point
constraints on complete components or the geometry of the associated
coexceptional curves.

Equations (8bj)--(8bm) are checked in
`verify_fixed_plane_conductor_filtration_image.py`.

### 1.12 A global two-puncture theorem for the conductor image

The first genuinely global restriction comes from combining the conductor
residue with the geometry of an étale plane map.  Suppose that
\[
e=(U,V):\mathbf A^2_{t,c}\longrightarrow\mathbf A^2
\]
is a hypothetical Darboux pair in \(R\), and let
\[
C=\{D=0\}.
\]
The parametrization
\[
c=\frac{v^2}{9},\qquad t=\frac{3(v+2)}{v^2}
\tag{8bn}
\]
identifies \(C\) with \(\mathbf G_m\).  On \(C\), every element of \(R\)
is invariant under \(v\mapsto-v\).  More explicitly,
\[
a=\frac{4-9c}{27c^2},\qquad
b=\frac{4-3c}{3c},\qquad c=c,
\tag{8bo}
\]
and the restriction of \(R\) is the full Laurent ring
\(\mathbf Q[c,c^{-1}]\), because \(b+1=4/(3c)\).

Put
\[
u(c)=U|_C,\qquad w(c)=V|_C
\]
and let \(\Gamma\) be the closure of the image of \(C\) under \(e\).
The map
\[
h:\mathbf G_{m,c}\longrightarrow\Gamma,\qquad c\longmapsto(u(c),w(c))
\tag{8bp}
\]
is generically finite and unramified.  Indeed, \(e\) is étale, so its
differential cannot kill the tangent to the smooth curve \(C\), and
\(dc/dv=2v/9\) is nonzero on \(\mathbf G_m\).

Let \(\widetilde\Gamma\) be the normalization of \(\Gamma\), and complete
it to a smooth projective curve \(\overline\Gamma\).  The inclusion
\[
\mathbf Q(\Gamma)\subset\mathbf Q(c)
\]
and Lüroth's theorem give \(\overline\Gamma\simeq\mathbf P^1\).
The map (8bp) extends to a finite map
\[
\overline h:\mathbf P^1_c\longrightarrow\mathbf P^1.
\]
Every point of
\(\overline\Gamma\setminus\widetilde\Gamma\) has all of its inverse
images in \(\{0,\infty\}\).  Hence there are at most two such points.
There is at least one because \(\Gamma\) is affine.  Thus
\[
\widetilde\Gamma\simeq\mathbf A^1
\quad\hbox{or}\quad
\widetilde\Gamma\simeq\mathbf G_m.
\tag{8bq}
\]
Since \(\overline h\) is unramified away from \(0,\infty\), the
Riemann--Hurwitz formula gives the familiar two-branch-point
classification.  In a coordinate \(s\) on the normalization,
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\widetilde\Gamma\simeq\mathbf A^1:
&s=\beta+\alpha c^{\pm\delta},\\[2mm]
\widetilde\Gamma\simeq\mathbf G_m:
&s=\alpha c^{\pm\delta},
\end{array}
\qquad \alpha\ne0,\quad\delta\ge1.
\tag{8br}
\]

The affine-line alternative is incompatible with the exact conductor
residue (8h).  In that alternative, regular functions on \(\Gamma\)
pull back to polynomials in \(s\).  Therefore \(u,w\) are polynomials
in \(\beta+\alpha c^\delta\), or both are polynomials in
\(\beta+\alpha c^{-\delta}\).  In either case every monomial of
\(u\,dw\) has exponent
\[
\delta(i+j)-1\quad\hbox{or}\quad-\delta(i+j)-1
\]
with \(i,j\ge0\); exponent \(-1\) can occur only when the differentiated
monomial is constant, and then its coefficient is zero.  Hence
\[
\operatorname {Res}_{c=0}(u\,dw)=0,
\]
contrary to (8h).  We have proved
\[
\boxed{\quad
\widetilde\Gamma\simeq\mathbf G_m,\qquad
h:\mathbf G_{m,c}\to\widetilde\Gamma
\text{ is a finite étale power cover.}\quad}
\tag{8bs}
\]
If its degree is \(\delta\), then the original conductor map
\(C_v\to\Gamma\) has degree \(2\delta\).  Moreover, after choosing the
coordinate \(s\),
\[
\operatorname {Res}_{c=0}(u\,dw)
=\pm\delta\,\operatorname {Res}_{s=0}(\bar u\,d\bar w),
\tag{8bt}
\]
where \(u=\bar u(s)\), \(w=\bar w(s)\).  Thus the nonzero residue is
exactly the obstruction to a one-puncture conductor image.

There is a compatible weak-type consequence.  Choose a generic
primitive element \(\pi\in R\) for
\(\mathbf Q(t,c)/\mathbf Q(U,V)\), and let
\[
Z_\pi=\overline{\operatorname {Im}(\pi,U,V)}\subset\mathbf A^3.
\]
Every \(\pi\in R\) has the same value at the two conductor points
\(v\) and \(-v\), so \(Z_\pi\) is nonnormal, hence singular, at the
generic point of the image of \(C\).  Thus \(C\) is a divisorial
component of
\[
(\pi,e)^{-1}\bigl(\operatorname {Sing}Z_\pi\bigr).
\tag{8bu}
\]
Corollary 25.7 of Borisov--Gabber--Vasiu says that a nonautomorphic
characteristic-zero Keller map has weak type exactly \(2\), not weak
type at most \(1\).  Consequently, for every such generic primitive
\(\pi\), at least one divisorial component \(Y\) of (8bu) must fail
the saturation condition:
\[
\boxed{\quad e^{-1}(e(Y))\ne Y.\quad}
\tag{8bv}
\]
Equivalently, there is another irreducible curve \(Y'\ne Y\) with
\(e(Y')=e(Y)\).  This is an affine collision/coexceptional-curve
constraint on every primitive graph.

One caveat is essential.  The conductor collision by itself does not
prove that \(Y=C\) in (8bv): both branches \(v\) and \(-v\) belong to
the same irreducible curve \(C\), so it is possible that
\(e^{-1}(\Gamma)=C\) while a different graph-singular divisor supplies
the weak-type-\(2\) failure.  Likewise, Borisov's projective
coexceptional-curve theorem concerns images of dicritical curves at
infinity; identifying one of those curves with \(C\) requires a further
compactification argument.  The exact new target is therefore to combine
(8bs) with (8bv), not to assume that (8bv) already occurs over
\(\Gamma\).

There is an exact pullback factorization, but it does not remove this
caveat.  If \(F(X,Y)\) is a reduced irreducible equation for \(\Gamma\),
then
\[
F(U,V)=\lambda D H_1\cdots H_r
\tag{8bw}
\]
in \(\overline{\mathbf Q}[t,c]\), where \(\lambda\ne0\), the \(H_i\)
are distinct irreducibles, and none is associated to \(D\).  The
divisibility by \(D\) follows from the definition of \(\Gamma\).
The factorization is squarefree because an étale morphism pulls a
reduced Cartier divisor back to a reduced Cartier divisor.  The curves
\(H_i=0\), if present, are exactly the additional affine components
mapping into \(\Gamma\).

If \(\Gamma\) is smooth, étaleness makes its full inverse image smooth.
Consequently the components in (8bw) are pairwise disjoint; in
particular, no \(H_i\) can meet \(C\).  If \(\Gamma\) is singular, an
intersection \(C\cap\{H_i=0\}\) can occur only over a singular point of
\(\Gamma\).  Thus neither (8bw) nor the nonzero residue forces an extra
component to attach to the conductor.

The limitation has a sharp torus countermodel.  On
\(\mathbf G_m^2\), consider the finite étale map
\[
(x,y)\longmapsto (X,Y)=(x^2,y^2)
\]
and the smooth target curve
\[
\Gamma=\{XY=1\}\simeq\mathbf G_m.
\]
Then
\[
X Y-1=x^2y^2-1=(xy-1)(xy+1).
\tag{8bx}
\]
The two inverse-image components are disjoint, and each maps to
\(\Gamma\) by the degree-two power map.  On either component,
\[
X=s,\qquad Y=s^{-1},\qquad
\operatorname {Res}_{s=0}(X\,dY)=-1\ne0.
\tag{8by}
\]
This model has exactly the étale, squarefree, two-puncture,
even-degree, and nonzero-residue features used above, yet its extra
component does not meet the chosen one.  It is not a counterexample on
\(\mathbf A^2\); rather, it proves that any attachment theorem must use
the global \(\mathbf A^2\) compactification (such as Borisov's cyclic
quotient point), not only the affine factorization and conductor
residue.

The conductor identities and the Laurent-residue behavior in (8bn)--(8bt)
and the countermodel (8bx)--(8by) are checked in
`verify_fixed_plane_global_conductor_image.py`.

### 1.13 An affine-coexceptional/dicritical-image dichotomy

The two-puncture theorem also gives a clean global dichotomy that does
not depend on choosing a primitive graph.  Work over \(\mathbf C\), and
let \(S_e\subset\mathbf A^2\) be the nonproper-value set of the
generically finite polynomial map \(e=(U,V)\).  Thus \(e\) is finite
over \(\mathbf A^2\setminus S_e\), and the irreducible components of
\(S_e\) are images of dicritical curves in a resolution at infinity.

Suppose first that the conductor image has no other affine inverse-image
component:
\[
e^{-1}(\Gamma)=C\qquad\text{set-theoretically}.
\tag{8bz}
\]
Then \(\Gamma\) is smooth.  Indeed, (8bs) says that \(C\to\Gamma\) is
finite and surjective, while étaleness identifies the germ of the
reduced inverse image of \(\Gamma\) with the germ of \(\Gamma\).
Under (8bz) that inverse-image germ is the smooth divisor \(C\), so every
point of \(\Gamma\) is smooth.  Consequently both \(C\) and \(\Gamma\)
are isomorphic to \(\mathbf G_m\).

If in addition
\[
S_e\subseteq\Gamma,
\tag{8ca}
\]
then restriction gives a finite étale cover
\[
\mathbf A^2\setminus C\longrightarrow
\mathbf A^2\setminus\Gamma
\tag{8cb}
\]
of degree \(\deg(e)\).  Compactly supported topological Euler
characteristics give
\[
\chi_c(\mathbf A^2\setminus C)
=1-\chi_c(\mathbf G_m)=1
=\chi_c(\mathbf A^2\setminus\Gamma).
\]
Euler characteristic is multiplicative under a finite étale cover, so
(8cb) forces \(\deg(e)=1\).  A birational étale polynomial self-map of
\(\mathbf A^2\) is an open immersion by Zariski's Main Theorem, hence
cannot contain the verified pairs of distinct conductor points in one
fiber.  This is a contradiction.

Therefore every fixed-plane Darboux pair must obey
\[
\boxed{\quad
e^{-1}(\Gamma)\supsetneq C
\quad\text{or}\quad
S_e\text{ has an irreducible component }\Lambda\ne\Gamma.
\quad}
\tag{8cc}
\]
The first alternative supplies an affine coexceptional curve over the
conductor image.  The second supplies a genuinely different dicritical
image at infinity.  The torus countermodel (8bx) shows that an affine
coexceptional component in the first alternative need not meet \(C\);
Borisov's stronger attachment conclusion can only enter after locating
the relevant cyclic quotient point on a projective compactification.

This dichotomy is stronger than the raw weak-type statement in one
specific respect: it names the conductor image \(\Gamma\).  It is weaker
in another: it does not yet prohibit either alternative.  A viable
global continuation is to compactify the explicit curve \(D=0\), track
its two places at infinity through the resolution of \(e\), and test
whether the second alternative in (8cc) is compatible with the connected
dicritical tree and Borisov's ample-ramification/coexceptional-attachment
theorems.

### 1.14 The two marked conductor arms at infinity

The projective closure of \(C\) supplies explicit boundary data for that
continuation.  In homogeneous coordinates \([T:C:Z]\), its equation is
\[
\overline D=
9C^2T^2-12CTZ^2-9CZ^3+4Z^4=0.
\tag{8cd}
\]
It meets \(Z=0\) at exactly the two points
\[
P_0=[1:0:0],\qquad P_\infty=[0:1:0].
\tag{8ce}
\]
They are the two places \(v=0,\infty\) of the normalization (8bn).

The local singularity types are also exact.  At \(P_0\), use \(T=1\)
and put
\[
Y=3C-2Z^2.
\]
Then
\[
\overline D=Y^2-3YZ^3-6Z^5,
\tag{8cf}
\]
so the unique branch has characteristic leading pair \((2,5)\).
At \(P_\infty\), use \(C=1\); then
\[
\overline D=9T^2-12TZ^2-9Z^3+4Z^4,
\tag{8cg}
\]
whose unique branch has leading pair \((2,3)\).  Thus the embedded
projective conductor is a rational quartic with an \(A_4\)-type cusp on
one end and an \(A_2\)-type cusp on the other.

The minimal simple-normal-crossing resolutions of the pairs consisting
of the cusp and the line at infinity are small.  Write \(L\) for the
strict transform of \(Z=0\).

At the \((2,5)\) end, four successive point blowups create
\(E_1,E_2,E_3,E_4\).  The final incidence graph, with the strict
conductor shown by an arrow, is
\[
L\;-\;E_1\;-\;E_2\;-\;E_4\;-\;E_3,
\qquad
\overline C\longrightarrow E_4.
\tag{8cdi}
\]
The self-intersections, in creation order, are
\[
(E_1^2,E_2^2,E_3^2,E_4^2)=(-2,-3,-2,-1).
\tag{8cdj}
\]
The multiplicities in the total pullback of \(\overline C\) and of the
line \(Z=0\) are respectively
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
&E_1&E_2&E_3&E_4\\ \hline
\pi^*\overline C&2&4&5&10\\
\pi^*(Z=0)&1&1&1&2.
\end{array}
\tag{8cdk}
\]
The last blowup is required because after the third one the strict
conductor passes through \(E_2\cap E_3\); it separates the resulting
triple point.

At the \((2,3)\) end, three blowups create \(F_1,F_2,F_3\), with
\[
L\;-\;F_1\;-\;F_3\;-\;F_2,
\qquad
\overline C\longrightarrow F_3,
\tag{8cdl}
\]
\[
(F_1^2,F_2^2,F_3^2)=(-3,-2,-1),
\tag{8cdm}
\]
and
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrr}
&F_1&F_2&F_3\\ \hline
\pi^*\overline C&2&3&6\\
\pi^*(Z=0)&1&1&2.
\end{array}
\tag{8cdn}
\]
Here the third blowup separates the strict conductor from the crossing
\(F_1\cap F_2\).  Since the first blowup at each end occurs at a
different point of the original line at infinity, \(L^2=1-2=-1\)
before any further blowups needed to resolve \(e\).

The augmented-canonical labels used by Borisov are determined at the
same time.  The original line has label \(-2\); blowing up a smooth point
on a boundary component of label \(a\) creates label \(a+1\), while
blowing up an intersection of labels \(a,b\) creates label \(a+b\).
Therefore
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
&E_1&E_2&E_3&E_4\\ \hline
\overline K\text{-label}&-1&0&1&1,
\end{array}
\qquad
\begin{array}{c|rrr}
&F_1&F_2&F_3\\ \hline
\overline K\text{-label}&-1&0&-1.
\end{array}
\tag{8cdo}
\]
In particular, the cusp resolution itself does not determine the map
types: type \(1\) requires a negative even label and type \(3\) requires
a positive label, but curves of type \(2\) or \(4\) have no sign
classification strong enough to decide these seven vertices.

After choosing the normalization coordinate \(s\) of \(\Gamma\), (8br)
and \(c=v^2/9\) give
\[
s=\alpha\,9^{\mp\delta}v^{\pm2\delta}.
\tag{8ch}
\]
Hence the completed map
\[
\overline C\simeq\mathbf P^1_v\longrightarrow
\overline{\widetilde\Gamma}\simeq\mathbf P^1_s
\]
is totally ramified with index \(2\delta\) at both \(v=0\) and
\(v=\infty\), and nowhere else.  The two points lie over distinct points
of the original source line at infinity, so they determine two marked
arms in every resolution of the polynomial map.

This is the concrete interface with the projective coexceptional
theorems.  Any resolution-based contradiction must accommodate
simultaneously

1. the \((2,5)\) arm,
2. the \((2,3)\) arm,
3. total conductor-cover ramification \(2\delta\) at both arms, and
4. one of the two alternatives in (8cc).

The label \(2\delta\) in (8ch) is a ramification label on the restriction
of the map to the strict conductor.  It does **not** determine the
divisorial ramification index, or even the Borisov type, of \(E_4\) or
\(F_3\).  A boundary divisor through either marked point may map to a
point of the target line at infinity (type \(2\)); total ramification of
the transverse affine curve is compatible with that behavior.

This observation produces a compatible labeled-tree countermodel to the
desired attachment inference.  Label the connected subtree consisting of
\(L\) and the seven vertices in (8cdi)--(8cdn) as type \(2\).  The only
final vertices of the bare cusp resolution are \(E_4\), of label \(1\),
and \(F_3\), of label \(-1\).  Blow up a generic point of \(E_4\); the
new label-\(2\) curve can be a final type-\(3\) curve.  At \(F_3\), two
successive generic blowups create labels \(0,1\); take the label-\(0\)
curve as type \(2\) and the label-\(1\) child as a final type-\(3\)
curve.  Give neither type-\(3\) curve a type-\(4\) tail.

For a type-\(1\) branch, blow up the intersection of
\(L(-2)\) and \(E_1(-1)\), creating a label-\(-3\) type-\(2\) vertex,
then blow up a generic point of it.  The label-\(-2\) child can be a
final type-\(1\) curve.  This extended skeleton has a connected
type-\(2\) subtree, every final boundary curve is type \(1\) or \(3\),
all type-\(1\) and type-\(3\) branches meet the type-\(2\) subtree, its
type-\(1\) label is negative even, its type-\(3\) labels are positive,
and no type-\(3\) curve has a cyclic quotient point.  Therefore
Borisov's Theorem 3.6 (“a type-\(3\) curve containing a cyclic quotient
point has a coexceptional curve through that point”) is vacuous on this
labeling.  The weak-type-\(2\) requirement can likewise be supplied by a
primitive-graph collision divisor away from the conductor arms.

This is a compatibility skeleton, not a realization by a Keller map:
the global canonical labels, intersection equations, and ample
ramification divisor have not been assigned.  It is nevertheless enough
to stop the proposed direct inference.  The exact hypotheses of the
available attachment theorem do not force either \((2,5)\) or \((2,3)\)
arm to contain its cyclic quotient point.  Proving such an incidence
would require a new global theorem, not a longer resolution of these
two cusps.

The blowup transforms, multiplicity recurrences, self-intersections, and
incidence graphs are checked in
`verify_fixed_plane_conductor_arm_resolution.py`.

## 2. Every linear target Hamiltonian is excluded in all degrees

Let

\[
U=\alpha a+\beta b+\gamma c.
\]

Its normalization derivatives are

\[
U_c=q(t)=\gamma-\alpha t^3-3\beta t^2,
\]

\[
U_t=\ell(t)-c\,k(t),\quad
\ell=\alpha(1+2t)+4\beta,\quad
k=3t(\alpha t+2\beta).
\tag{9}
\]

If \(q(t_0)=0\) and \(k(t_0)\ne0\), choosing
\(c=\ell(t_0)/k(t_0)\) gives a critical point of \(U\).  Such a critical
point makes \(\{U,V\}=1\) impossible for every polynomial \(V\).

Over an algebraically closed characteristic-zero field, every nonzero
linear combination other than a scalar multiple of \(a,b,c\) has such a
critical point.  Here is the short classification.  If
\(\alpha=0,\beta\ne0\), then
\(q=\gamma-3\beta t^2\); unless \(\gamma=0\), it has a nonzero root where
\(k\ne0\).  If \(\alpha\ne0\), scale it to one and put
\(r=\beta/\alpha\).  Were all three roots of

\[
q=-t^3-3rt^2+\gamma
\]

supported on the two roots \(0,-2r\) of \(k\), then for some
\(0\le m\le3\)

\[
q=-t^m(t+2r)^{3-m}.
\]

For \(m=0,1\), the right side has a nonzero \(t\)-coefficient when
\(r\ne0\); for \(m=2,3\), its \(t^2\)-coefficient disagrees with
\(-3r\).  Hence \(r=0,\gamma=0\), giving \(U=a\).  The remaining
critical-point-free directions are \(b\) and \(c\).

These three exceptional directions also have no mate in \(R\):

- For \(U=a\), write \(V=\sum_{j=0}^n v_j(t)c^j\).  The top \(c^n\)
  coefficient of \(\{a,V\}=1\) gives
  \(t v_n'=3n v_n\), so \(v_n=K t^{3n}\).  Subtracting a scalar multiple
  of \(a^n\) lowers the \(c\)-degree.  Repetition leaves
  \(t^3v_0'=1\), impossible for polynomial \(v_0\).
- For \(U=b\), the same argument gives
  \(t v_n'=2n v_n\); subtracting a multiple of \(b^n\) eventually leaves
  \(3t^2v_0'=1\), again impossible.  These two arguments exclude partners
  even in the full normalization \(\mathbf Q[t,c]\).
- For \(U=c\), a constant bracket forces
  \(V=\lambda t+f(c)\).  Such a polynomial is not in \(R\): the exact
  collision has equal \(c\) but distinct \(t\), while every element of
  \(R\) takes equal values there.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{\text{No nonconstant linear }U(A,B,C)
\text{ has a polynomial Darboux mate of any degree.}}
\]

This upgrades the earlier degree-eight bounded computation to an all-degree
theorem.  A successful pair in the fixed-plane ring, if one exists, must
start with a genuinely nonlinear critical-point-free Hamiltonian.

## 3. The first nonlinear submersion

A sparse quadratic scan found one additional critical-point-free element:

\[
U=a(1+b)
=t(1+t-ct^2)(3+4t-3ct^2).
\tag{10}
\]

The exact gradient ideal is the unit ideal, so a critical-point argument
does not dispose of it.  Nevertheless it has no polynomial mate even in
the full normalization.  If

\[
V=v_n(t)c^n+\text{lower powers of }c,
\]

the coefficient of \(c^{n+1}\) in \(\{U,V\}\) is

\[
3t^4\bigl(5n v_n-2t v_n'\bigr).
\]

It can vanish with polynomial \(v_n\ne0\) only when \(n\) is even, in
which case \(v_n=K t^{5n/2}\).  This is exactly the leading term of a
multiple of \(U^{n/2}\).  Subtracting that invariant lowers the
\(c\)-degree.  Repetition leaves \(V=v_0(t)\), but then

\[
\{U,V\}
=-t^3(6ct^2-7t-6)v_0'(t),
\]

which cannot be a nonzero constant.  Thus (10) is excluded in all partner
degrees.  The earlier modular search independently found rank gaps through
target degree eight.

## 4. General nonsquare leading-coefficient obstruction

The preceding recurrence is not special to (10).  Let

\[
U=u_2(t)c^2+u_1(t)c+u_0(t)
\]

and suppose \(u_2\ne0\).  If
\[
V=v_m(t)c^m+\cdots
\]
has constant nonzero bracket with \(U\), the top \(c^{m+1}\) coefficient
must vanish:

\[
m u_2'v_m-2u_2v_m'=0.
\tag{11}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\left(\frac{v_m^2}{u_2^m}\right)'=0.
\]

If \(u_2\) is not a square in the algebraic closure of
\(\mathbf Q[t]\), unique factorization forces \(m\) to be even and
\(v_m\) to be a scalar multiple of \(u_2^{m/2}\).  Subtracting the
corresponding multiple of \(U^{m/2}\) lowers the \(c\)-degree without
changing the bracket.  Iteration leaves \(V=v_0(t)\), for which

\[
\{U,V\}=-(2u_2c+u_1)v_0'
\]

cannot be a nonzero constant.  Therefore:

\[
\boxed{u_2(t)\text{ nonsquare }\Longrightarrow
U\text{ has no polynomial Darboux mate}.}
\tag{12}
\]

This reduces every genuinely quadratic target Hamiltonian to the thin
square-leading locus.  In particular (10) has \(u_2=3t^5\), so its
all-degree exclusion is an instance of (12).

The same argument has a useful arbitrary-degree form.  If
\[
U=u_d(t)c^d+\cdots,\qquad V=v_n(t)c^n+\cdots,
\]
then the top bracket coefficient is
\[
n u_d'v_n-d u_dv_n'=0.
\]
For every irreducible factor \(p\) of \(u_d\), taking its valuation gives
\[
d\,\operatorname{ord}_p(v_n)
=n\,\operatorname{ord}_p(u_d).
\]
Consequently, if
\[
\gcd\left(d,\{\operatorname{ord}_p(u_d):p\mid u_d\}\right)=1,
\tag{12a}
\]
then \(d\mid n\), \(v_n\) is a scalar multiple of \(u_d^{n/d}\), and
subtracting the corresponding multiple of \(U^{n/d}\) lowers the
\(c\)-degree.  Iteration reduces a putative mate to \(v_0(t)\), which is
impossible unless \(d=1\) and \(u_1(t)\) is constant.

This primitive-leading obstruction excludes an infinite boundary family:
\[
U=a(b+1)^m,\qquad m\ge0.
\tag{12b}
\]
Indeed, \(d=m+1\) and
\[
u_d=(-1)^{m+1}3^m t^{2m+3},
\qquad
\gcd(m+1,2m+3)=1.
\]
Thus no member of (12b) has a polynomial mate of any degree, even in the
full normalization.  This includes \(U=a\) and the first nonlinear
submersion (10).  The exact recurrence checks are in
`verify_fixed_plane_primitive_leading.py`.

## 5. A square-leading submersion family, excluded in all degrees

The square-leading locus contains a genuine one-parameter family of
submersions:

\[
U_r=(b+1)^2+ra
\tag{13}
\]

(irrelevant additive constants are suppressed).  Direct critical-ideal
calculation shows that generic \(r\) is critical-point-free, so neither the
critical-point argument nor (12) applies.

Nevertheless, the Hamiltonian equation for (13) is exactly solvable.  Put

\[
H=b+1.
\]

Solving for \(c\) after inverting \(t\) gives

\[
c=\frac{3+4t-H}{3t^2},\qquad
a=\frac{t(H-t)}3,
\]

and hence

\[
U_r=H^2+\frac r3t(H-t).
\tag{14}
\]

Every polynomial in the normalization becomes an element of
\(\mathbf Q[t,t^{-1},H]\).  Since

\[
\frac{\partial H}{\partial c}=-3t^2,
\]

the equation \(\operatorname{Jac}_{t,c}(U_r,V)=1\) becomes

\[
\operatorname{Jac}_{t,H}(U_r,V)=-\frac1{3t^2}.
\tag{15}
\]

The Hamiltonian derivation of the homogeneous quadratic (14) preserves the
total grading \(\deg t=\deg H=1\).  Thus only the degree \(-2\) homogeneous
part of \(V\) can produce the right side of (15).  Every such Laurent
polynomial has the form

\[
t^{-2}f(s),\qquad s=H/t,\quad f\in\mathbf Q[s].
\]

Substitution into (15) gives

\[
2\bigl(A(s)f(s)\bigr)'=-\frac13,\qquad
A(s)=s^2+\frac r3s-\frac r3.
\tag{16}
\]

After integration, (16) would require

\[
A(s)f(s)=-\frac{s}{6}+K.
\]

This is impossible because \(A\) is monic quadratic and \(f\) is a
polynomial.  Therefore (13) has no polynomial mate for any \(r\), in any
degree.  The verifier is `verify_fixed_plane_square_family.py`.

## 6. Complete quadratic--quadratic exclusion

There is also a complete, exact obstruction when both target polynomials
have degree at most two.  Let

\[
W=\langle A,B,C,A^2,AB,AC,B^2,BC,C^2\rangle.
\]

For \(U,V\in W\), introduce their 36 wedge coefficients
\(w_{ij}=u_iv_j-u_jv_i\).  The equation
\(\{U,V\}=1\) is linear in \(w\).  Exact row reduction gives rank 26 and a
ten-dimensional affine solution space.  Every point of that affine space
satisfies

\[
\begin{gathered}
w_{36}=w_{37}=w_{68}=w_{78}=0,\\
w_{38}=-\frac94,\qquad w_{67}=-\frac14.
\end{gathered}
\tag{17}
\]

However, a wedge \(u\wedge v\) is decomposable and must obey the Plücker
relation

\[
w_{36}w_{78}-w_{37}w_{68}+w_{38}w_{67}=0.
\]

The forced values (17) make its left side \(9/16\).  This contradiction
excludes every pair with
\(\deg_{A,B,C}U,\deg_{A,B,C}V\le2\), over characteristic zero.  The verifier
is `verify_fixed_plane_quadratic_pair.py`.

## 7. Complete quadratic--cubic exclusion

The leading coefficient of a general quadratic target Hamiltonian is

\[
u_2(t)=
q_{AA}t^6+3q_{AB}t^5+9q_{BB}t^4-q_{AC}t^3
-3q_{BC}t^2+q_{CC}.
\tag{18}
\]

The six displayed powers are distinct, so \(u_2=0\) forces the entire
quadratic homogeneous part to vanish; this is the already-excluded linear
case.  The nonsquare case is excluded in all degrees by (12).

On the remaining square locus, write

\[
u_2=(h_0+h_1t+h_2t^2+h_3t^3)^2.
\]

The missing \(t^1\) coefficient forces \(h_0h_1=0\).  Up to rescaling
\((U,V)\), the nonzero square root is covered by four affine charts:

\[
\begin{array}{c}
h_0=0,\ h_1=1;\\
h_0=1,\ h_1=0;\\
h_0=h_1=0,\ h_2=1;\\
h_0=h_1=h_2=0,\ h_3=1.
\end{array}
\]

For each chart, `verify_fixed_plane_quadratic_cubic.py` constructs the
fully general square-leading quadratic \(U\), the fully general target
polynomial \(V\) of degree at most three, and every coefficient equation
in \(\{U,V\}=1\).  Singular returns the exact Gröbner basis \([1]\) over
\(\mathbf Q\) on all four charts.  Together with (12) and the linear
theorem, this proves

\[
\boxed{\deg_{A,B,C}U\le2,\quad
\deg_{A,B,C}V\le3
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\{U,V\}\ne1.}
\tag{19}
\]

The result is symmetric in the pair, so it also covers degrees
\((3,2)\).  It strictly strengthens the quadratic--quadratic Plücker
calculation, which remains useful as a short independent certificate.

## 8. An all-degree boundary restriction in \(\mathbf Q[a,b]\)

There is a useful all-degree restriction for every Hamiltonian that does
not use the third target generator \(c\).  Put \(H=b+1\).  After inverting
\(t\),
\[
a=\frac{t(H-t)}3,\qquad b=H-1,\qquad
c=\frac{3+4t-H}{3t^2},
\]
and
\[
\operatorname{Jac}_{t,H}(U,V)=-\frac1{3t^2}
\tag{20}
\]
for a normalized Darboux pair.

Let \(U\in\mathbf Q[a,b]\), so its expression in \(t,H\) is polynomial,
and write a possible normalization mate as the finite Laurent polynomial
\[
V=\sum_{j=-N}^M t^jv_j(H),\qquad v_{-N}\ne0.
\]
If \(u_0(H)=U(0,H-1)\), the coefficient of \(t^{-N-1}\) on the left of
(20) is
\[
N u_0'(H)v_{-N}(H).
\]
Thus, whenever \(u_0'\ne0\), necessarily \(N=1\) and
\[
u_0'(H)v_{-1}(H)=-\frac13.
\]
Both factors are polynomials, so \(u_0'\) is a nonzero constant.  Hence
\(U(0,b)=\lambda b+\text{constant}\).  Writing
\[
U=\lambda b+\text{constant}+aG(a,b),
\]
the next Laurent coefficient is a nonzero scalar times
\[
\frac d{dH}\left(\frac H3G(0,H-1)\right).
\]
It must vanish.  Polynomiality then forces \(G(0,b)=0\), so \(G\) is
divisible by \(a\).

If instead \(u_0'=0\), then
\[
U=\text{constant}+aG(a,b).
\]
A Darboux Hamiltonian is a submersion.  Along the component \(a=0\) whose
coordinate \(b\) is nonconstant, its differential is
\(G(0,b)\,da\).  In the normalization this component is
\[
H=b+1=t,\qquad t\ne0.
\]
Thus \(G(0,H-1)\) need only have no zeros on \(\mathbb G_m\), so it is
\(\gamma H^m\) for some \(m\ge0\) and \(\gamma\ne0\).  This puncture is
essential at the submersion stage: \(a(b+1)\) is a verified submersion.
Thus
\[
U=\text{constant}+\gamma a(b+1)^m+a^2G(a,b)
\]
with \(m\ge0\).

The Hamiltonian equation now supplies the missing restriction.  In
\((t,H)\)-coordinates,
\[
U=\text{constant}+\frac{\gamma}{3}tH^{m+1}+O(t^2).
\]
If \(V=t^{-N}v_{-N}(H)+\cdots\), the lowest bracket coefficient is
\[
\frac{\gamma}{3}\left(
H^{m+1}v_{-N}'+N(m+1)H^mv_{-N}
\right)t^{-N}.
\]
For \(N>2\) it would have to vanish, but its polynomial kernel is zero.
Hence \(N=2\), and comparison with (20) gives
\[
H^{m+1}v_{-2}'+2(m+1)H^mv_{-2}=-\frac1\gamma.
\]
For \(m\ge1\) the left side is divisible by \(H\), a contradiction.
Therefore \(m=0\).  Combining both boundary cases confines every possible
\(U\in\mathbf Q[a,b]\) to
\[
\boxed{
U=\text{constant}+\gamma a+a^2G(a,b)
\quad\text{or}\quad
U=\text{constant}+\lambda b+a^2G(a,b),
}
\tag{21}
\]
with \(\gamma\ne0\) or \(\lambda\ne0\), respectively.

There is one further all-degree restriction.  The nontrivial critical-value
curve of the map \((t,c)\mapsto(a,b)\) has parametrization
\[
a=\frac{t^2}{3},\qquad b=2t-1
\]
and equation
\[
\Delta=(b+1)^2-12a=0.
\]
On this curve, the two expressions in (21) restrict respectively to
\[
\text{constant}+\frac{\gamma t^2}{3}
  +\frac{t^4}{9}G\left(\frac{t^2}{3},2t-1\right)
\]
and
\[
\text{constant}+\lambda(2t-1)
  +\frac{t^4}{9}G\left(\frac{t^2}{3},2t-1\right).
\]
The derivative of either restriction can have no zero at \(t\ne0\).
After removing the unavoidable factor \(t\) in the first family, its
value at \(t=0\) is nonzero; the derivative in the second family is itself
nonzero at \(t=0\).  Any nonconstant polynomial remainder would have a
nonzero root over the algebraic closure.  Hence \(G\) vanishes on the
parabola and is divisible by \(\Delta\).  The necessary forms sharpen to
\[
\boxed{
U=\text{constant}+\gamma a+a^2\Delta K(a,b)
\quad\text{or}\quad
U=\text{constant}+\lambda b+a^2\Delta K(a,b).
}
\tag{22}
\]

The constant \(K\) stratum can also be closed exactly.  After rescaling,
write
\[
U_a=a+k a^2\Delta,\qquad U_b=b+k a^2\Delta.
\]
For \(k\ne0\), eliminating \(c\) from the two partial derivatives gives
\[
\operatorname{Res}_c((U_a)_t,(U_a)_c)
=5832k^3t^{39}(4kt^4-1)
\]
and
\[
\operatorname{Res}_c((U_b)_t,(U_b)_c)
=17496k^3t^{35}\bigl(8(kt^5)^2+396kt^5+27\bigr).
\]
Every nonzero \(k\) therefore has an explicit critical point over the
algebraic closure; the penultimate subresultants recover \(c\) and are
checked exactly in `verify_fixed_plane_constant_tail_critical.py`.
When \(k=0\), the all-degree recurrences for \(a\) and \(b\) already rule
out a mate.

The entire affine-\(K\) stratum is also closed.  Put
\[
K=k_0+k_1a+k_2(b+1).
\]
For both families in (22), the exact derivative resultant is a power of
\(t\) times a residual polynomial.  Saturated coefficient ideals on the
charts \(k_2\ne0\), \(k_2=0,\ k_0k_1\ne0\), and
\(k_2=k_0=0,\ k_1\ne0\) prove that this residual is never a monomial.
It therefore has a nonzero root over the algebraic closure.  Moreover,
the top normalization coefficient in both cases is
\[
[c^5]U=-9t^{12}(k_1t+3k_2),
\]
which has no multiple nonzero root.  Thus the resultant root cannot be
an artifact at \(c=\infty\): it is a finite common zero of \(U_t,U_c\).
The exact saturated Gröbner certificates are in
`verify_fixed_plane_affine_tail_critical.py`.

There is also an all-degree restriction on the remaining leading form.
If \(k=\deg K\) and \(K_k\) is its top homogeneous part, either family
in (22) has normalization \(c\)-degree \(d=k+4\) and leading coefficient
\[
[c^d]U=(-1)^k9t^{2k+10}K_k(t,3).
\]
The primitive-leading obstruction (12a) applies because
\[
\gcd(k+4,2k+10)=\gcd(k+4,2).
\]
Thus every odd-degree \(K\) is excluded outright.  When \(k\) is even,
the only way to evade (12a) is for every root multiplicity of
\(K_k(t,3)\) to be even, namely for \(K_k(t,3)\) to be a square over the
algebraic closure.

The first homogeneous square exception is closed independently.  For
\[
K=(ra+s(b+1))^2,
\]
both derivative resultants factor as
\[
t^N(rt+3s)^5R(t).
\]
The displayed linear factor is exactly the possible root at
\(c=\infty\).  On the chart \(rs\ne0\), the residual \(R\) has nonzero
constant and leading coefficients but zero \(t^6\)-coefficient, so it
cannot be a power of \(rt+3s\); it therefore has a different, finite
root.  The charts \(r=0\) and \(s=0\) are even more direct.  Hence every
homogeneous square quadratic tail has a critical point.  See
`verify_fixed_plane_homogeneous_square_tail_critical.py`.

In fact the entire quadratic exceptional locus is closed, including all
lower terms.  Write
\[
K=(ra+s(b+1))^2+k_0+k_1a+k_2(b+1),
\qquad (r,s)\ne(0,0).
\]
Every target point \((a,H)\) with \(a\ne0\) lifts to a finite
normalization point: solve \(t(H-t)=3a\), whose roots are nonzero, and
then use \(c=(3+4t-H)/(3t^2)\).  Hence it suffices below to produce a
target-coordinate critical point with \(a\ne0\).
On the chart \(s\ne0\), eliminating \(b+1\) from the two target-coordinate
partial derivatives gives
\[
2a^8s^2P_a(a),\qquad 2a^3s^2P_b(a).
\]
Their residual constant terms are \(128s^6\) and \(4s^4\).  Exact
saturated coefficient ideals prove that both residuals are nonconstant
for every parameter choice.  Hence each has a nonzero \(a\)-root, and
the nonvanishing leading derivative coefficients make it a finite
critical point.

When \(s=0\), necessarily \(r\ne0\).  If \(k_2\ne0\), the lower-degree
resultants have the same nonconstant/nonzero-constant property.  Finally
let \(s=k_2=0\), put \(K_0=r^2a^2+k_1a+k_0\), and
\(L=a^2K_0\).  In the \(a\)-branch, setting \(b+1=0\) leaves a degree-four
critical equation with constant \(1\) and leading coefficient \(-60r^2\).
In the \(b\)-branch, eliminating \(b+1\) gives
\[
N=L'-48aL^2L'-48L^3.
\]
This has degree \(12\).  At a root of \(L\) of multiplicity \(m\), it has
multiplicity exactly \(m-1\), so at most
\(\deg L-1=3\) of its roots lie on \(L=0\).  A remaining root yields a
finite critical point.  All resultants and saturated ideals are checked in
`verify_fixed_plane_quadratic_square_tail_critical.py`.

There is also a uniform all-degree critical-point formula for every
monomial tail
\[
K=a^p(b+1)^q,\qquad p,q\ge0.
\]
Writing \(H=b+1\), the \(a\)-branch derivative in \(H\) factors, for
\(q\ge1\), as
\[
a^{p+2}H^{q-1}\bigl((q+2)H^2-12qa\bigr).
\]
On its nonzero component the other derivative becomes
\[
1-\frac{12(2p+q+6)}{q+2}a^{p+2}H^q=0,
\]
which always has an algebraic solution.  For \(q=0\), simply taking
\(H=0\) leaves
\[
1-12(p+3)a^{p+2}=0.
\]
In the \(b\)-branch, the first derivative instead has the nonzero
component
\[
(p+2)H^2-12(p+3)a=0,
\]
and the remaining equation again has the form \(1+\eta a^iH^j=0\) with
\(\eta\ne0\).  Thus every monomial \(K\), in every degree, has a critical
point in both families.  The uniform identities are checked in
`verify_fixed_plane_monomial_tail_critical.py`.

The monomial calculation extends to every nonzero univariate tail
\(K=K(a)\).  In the \(a\)-branch, \(H=0\) again kills the \(H\)-derivative,
while the other derivative is
\[
1-36a^2K(a)-12a^3K'(a).
\]
It has constant \(1\), and if \(d=\deg K\), its leading coefficient is
\(-12(d+3)\operatorname{lc}(K)\), so it has a nonzero root.  In the
\(b\)-branch put \(L=a^2K(a)\).  The same elimination as above gives
\[
N=L'-48aL^2L'-48L^3.
\]
If \(D=\deg L\), then \(\deg N=3D\), while its roots supported on \(L=0\)
have total multiplicity at most \(D-1\).  Hence a root off \(L=0\)
produces a critical point.  The identities are checked in
`verify_fixed_plane_univariate_a_tail_critical.py`.

For the \(b\)-branch, every univariate \(K=K(H)\) is also excluded.  Here
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial a}
\left(H+a^2(H^2-12a)K(H)\right)
=2aK(H)(H^2-18a).
\]
On the component \(a=H^2/18\), the other derivative is
\[
1+\frac{H^5}{972}\left(6K(H)+HK'(H)\right).
\]
For nonzero \(K\) this is nonconstant with constant term \(1\), so it has
a nonzero algebraic root and hence gives a critical point.  See
`verify_fixed_plane_univariate_h_b_tail_critical.py`.

The analogous \(a\)-branch is closed as well, although its proof is less
immediate.  Write \(k=K(H)\), \(k'=dK/dH\), and
\[
F=a+a^2(H^2-12a)k(H).
\]
At a critical point with \(k'\ne0\), the \(H\)-derivative gives
\[
a=\frac{H(Hk'+2k)}{12k'}.
\]
After this substitution, the other derivative is
\[
F_a=\frac{E(H)}{12k'(H)^2},\qquad
E=12k'^2-H^2k(Hk'+2k)(Hk'+6k).
\tag{25}
\]
If \(d=\deg k\ge1\), then \(\deg E=3d+2\).  A root of \(E\) at which
\(k'\ne0\) automatically has both \(H\ne0\) and \(Hk'+2k\ne0\), so it
produces \(a\ne0\) and a finite critical point.

It remains to prove that not every root of \(E\) can lie on \(k'=0\).
Modulo \(k'\),
\[
E\equiv-12H^2k^3.
\]
Thus a nonzero common root is a multiple root of \(k\).  If its
multiplicity in \(k\) is \(m\), its exact multiplicity in \(E\) is
\(2m-2\).  The same formula holds at \(H=0\) when \(k(0)=0\).
Only the case \(k(0)\ne0,\ k'(0)=0\) needs an additional local bound:
\[
\operatorname{ord}_{H=0}E\le d+2.
\tag{26}
\]

For completeness, (26) follows from an explicit description of the two
formal branches.  Choose \(\mu^2=k(0)\), put \(x=\mu H^2\), and write
\(k=k(0)y(x)\).  The exact equation \(E=0\) becomes
\[
12y'^2=y(xy'+y)(xy'+3y).
\tag{27}
\]
The branch with \(y'(0)=1/2\) has the rational parametrization
\[
x=\frac{12(T-1)}{T(T+3)},\qquad
y=\frac{T(T+3)^2}{16};
\]
the other branch is obtained by \(x\mapsto-x\).  With \(u=T-1\),
\[
u=\frac{x(1+u)(4+u)}{12},\qquad
y=1+\frac32u+\frac9{16}u^2+\frac1{16}u^3.
\]
The recurrence for \(u\) has strictly positive rational coefficients,
so every coefficient of the positive branch is nonzero, and the negative
branch only alternates their signs.  The quadratic equation for \(k'\)
has these two branches separated to exact \(H\)-order one.  Therefore a
degree-\(d\) polynomial can agree with either branch only until its first
omitted nonzero even coefficient, of \(H\)-degree at most \(d+2\), proving
(26).

Now let \(j=\operatorname{ord}_0(k')\) when \(k(0)\ne0,\ k'(0)=0\).
The nonzero multiple roots consume at most \(d-1-j\) degrees of \(k'\),
so all forbidden roots of \(E\), counted with multiplicity, contribute at
most
\[
(d+2)+2(d-1-j)\le3d-2<3d+2.
\]
The cases \(k(0)=0\) or \(k'(0)\ne0\) have the still smaller bound
\(2d-2\).  Hence \(E\) has a root off \(k'=0\), as required.  A constant
nonzero \(k\) is handled directly by taking \(H=0\) and solving
\(1-36a^2k=0\).  See
`verify_fixed_plane_univariate_h_a_tail_critical.py`.

The first remaining homogeneous degree is closed as well.  If
\[
K=M_2(a,H)^2+k_0+k_1a+k_2H,\qquad
M_2=ra^2+saH+qH^2,
\]
then on \(q\ne0\) the two target-gradient resultants, after removing their
systematic powers of \(a\), have residual degrees \(28,35\) and respective
constant terms \(-5832q^7,-4q^5\).  Saturated positive-coefficient ideals
are units, so both residuals are nonconstant for every affine lower term.
On \(q=0,\ s\ne0\), the residual degrees are \(22,26\).  Their constants
can vanish, so a stronger calculation is used: for every possible retained
coefficient, the ideal generated by all the other coefficients becomes the
unit ideal after saturating by \(s\).  Neither residual can therefore
specialize to a monomial, and each has a nonzero root.  On \(q=s=0\) and
\(k_2\ne0\), residuals of degrees \(19,23\) have nonzero constants
\(-27k_2^2,-4k_2\) and saturated positive-coefficient ideals equal to the
unit ideal.  The final \(k_2=0\) chart is the already-closed univariate
tail \(K=r^2a^4+k_0+k_1a\).  Leading \(H\)-coefficients exclude
degree-drop artifacts in every chart.  Thus every quartic square-top tail
is excluded even after an arbitrary affine lower deformation; see
`verify_fixed_plane_quartic_affine_lower_critical.py`.  The earlier
constant-only calculation remains as a smaller independent check in
`verify_fixed_plane_homogeneous_quartic_square_tail_critical.py`.

Consequently any still-possible \(U\in\mathbf Q[a,b]\) must have one of
the forms (22), with even \(\deg K\ge4\) and \(K_k(t,3)\) square.  If
\(\deg K=4\), its lower-degree part must contain a term of degree at least
two; arbitrary affine lower terms are now excluded.  In every degree,
\(K\) must genuinely depend on both \(a\) and \(H=b+1\), since the two
univariate axes are now closed in both branches.

This is a necessary condition, not yet an all-degree exclusion.  It does,
however, eliminate at once every
quadratic submersion \(a+\alpha b^2+\beta b\) with \(\alpha\ne0\), including
families not covered by the homogeneous Laurent ODE (16).  The coefficient
identities are checked by `verify_fixed_plane_ab_boundary.py`.

## 9. Current live problem

The fixed-plane route remains a counterexample construction problem, now in
the exact form (8).  The next necessary step is to classify nonlinear
critical-point-free elements of \(R\), then solve their Hamiltonian equation.
Every linear Hamiltonian and the first nontrivial quadratic submersion are
now excluded in all degrees, every nonsquare-leading quadratic Hamiltonian
is excluded in all degrees, the family (13) is excluded in all degrees, and
every quadratic Hamiltonian is excluded against every partner through
target degree three.  Thus the unresolved quadratic case is confined to
square leading coefficient and a partner of degree at least four.  All
higher Hamiltonian degrees also remain open.

The exact verifier is `verify_fixed_source_plane_structure.py`; the bounded
search and quadratic-field collision verifier are
`search_fixed_source_plane.py`.
